My first PR went straight to master. No pipeline. No required approvals. Just a merge and a prayer. 

In my first role as a software engineer, we did not use strict agile practices yet and committed PRs directly to master. As the project matured, we utilized a DEV branch with better PR practices. Looking back, I didn't fully appreciate how risky that first approach was. But it reflected where the team was: early, scrappy, and providing value as we went.

About a year in, we introduced Scrum Poker. And that's when I started paying close attention to process.

On a diverse team — frontend, backend, QA, DevOps — Scrum Poker starts to break down. The range of concerns is too wide for anyone to give every ticket the understanding it deserves. Each engineer is focused on their own area and whatever their code interacts with. If there is one frontend engineer on the team, they get to guess their own ticket estimate. If there is one engineer specializing in a certain complex feature, they get to guess their own ticket estimate. Everyone else is just there to listen and hopefully agree.

It makes more sense to set up specialized sessions for the people who are actually concerned. General story review sessions where teams discuss how work connects across disciplines, paired with demo sessions at the end of a sprint to show what shipped and why it mattered. You stay aligned without pretending everyone understands everything.

That said, I don't think Scrum Poker is broken, just frequently misapplied. I've seen it work well, but the conditions have to be right.

**The team needs a similar skill set.** 
A room full of backend engineers can meaningfully estimate backend work. A mixed team cannot. Scrum Poker is not the time or place to teach frontend engineers about backend features and technology. 

**The team needs similar goals.**
On large multi-repo projects, engineers lose context quickly. Asking someone to estimate work in a codebase they rarely touch means asking them to carry context they don't have. Engineers do their best work with a focused set of problems. Context switching is a tax.

**Leadership has to understand that story points are estimates… and early warnings.** 
High story points signal more than effort. They reveal unknowns, steep learning curves, and blockers. When an engineer scores something high, that's a conversation starter, not a complaint. And when engineers consistently score high? That's a signal they need more context, more support, or clearer requirements. Technically savvy leadership reads between those numbers. Naïve managers just see a timeline slipping and place unnecessary pressure on the people trying to tell them something important. 

A game of poker should be interesting and engaging. When Scrum Poker starts to feel like it’s going quiet, that’s a signal. Good engineering leaders design processes around their teams, and know when to fold. The meetings should make the engineers feel ready to solve problems and be heard, not like they're sitting through yet another meeting with weird rules.
